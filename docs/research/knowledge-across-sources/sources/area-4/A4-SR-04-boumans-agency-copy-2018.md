## Evidence

- Abstract: "Results suggest that particularly online news is highly dependent on agency content, with the agency being responsible for up to 75% of the online news articles. Furthermore, a large part of the online news consists of verbatim agency copy, involving little or no editing."
- Literature review: "generally measured by counting the references to the agency in the credit line (...), which is a notoriously inaccurate measurement given news organizations' inconsistent attributing practices".
- Literature review: "Content is often subtly rewritten and either not attributed or erroneously attributed to a journalist".
- Method: "A systematic manual analysis of a subset of the data showed that a similarity score of .65 and above indicates a content overlap that is too high to be reasonably explained by chance: The majority of the content is identical."
- Method: "Churnalism index = 1 − [leva,b − (LIa − LIb)] / LIb".
- Method: "when the churnalism index > .7, the two texts are nearly identical; a score of 0 indicates that, while there is some resemblance in terms of topic and word usage, the media text differs substantially from the source text."
- Discussion: "that the agency and the newspaper independently of each other replicate the same source text, such as a press release. Although this possibility cannot be ruled out entirely, there are two arguments against this alternative explanation."
- Shows: a scalable method for verbatim and lightly edited reuse of agency copy, and the criticism of credit-line counting that motivated it.
- Does not show: a method that separates independent reporting from undisclosed reuse of a shared upstream source. It also does not measure frame or stance changes.
