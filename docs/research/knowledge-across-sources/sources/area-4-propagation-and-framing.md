# Area 4: Claim Propagation and Framing

This index covers assignment A4 of the survey. It sorts 17 source records in `sources/area-4/` by the question they answer. The question is this: when a downstream outlet reuses a primary report, how does the literature classify that reuse, and which methods detect each class? Each statement cites record IDs. Words in double quotes stand in the records' Evidence sections.

## State of the Art

The literature has no single scheme with the four classes verbatim, altered, reframed, and independently reported. Each class appears in a different research line, and each line has its own unit of analysis.

Text reuse detection on news is mature for verbatim and lightly edited copy. METER labels newspaper stories as wholly derived, partially derived, or non-derived from agency copy (A4-SR-01). Its classifier separates the two outer classes well, but it confuses the partially-derived class with both (A4-SR-01). Boumans et al. trace agency copy across a full year of Dutch news. They find that up to 75% of online articles come from the agency and that much of it is "verbatim agency copy" (A4-SR-04). Memetracker tracks quoted phrases across 1.6 million sites and groups their "mutational variants" (A4-SR-03). PAN-PC-10 models altered reuse through graded obfuscation, and its authors find that kinds of reuse "do not differ very much under n-gram models" (A4-SR-02).

Detection of altered meaning is younger. Sumner et al. code the strength of causal claims by hand across paper, press release, and news (A4-SR-14). Wright et al. score information change between a finding and its retellings and train models on 6,000 pairs (A4-SR-13).

Framing research measures the frame of one text. The Media Frames Corpus labels articles with 15 generic dimensions (A4-SR-09). SemEval-2023 Task 3 uses 14 of them in nine languages (A4-SR-10). Both sources report imperfect agreement among annotators (A4-SR-09, A4-SR-10).

Stance detection gives a text-to-target label. SemEval-2016 labels tweets as favour, against, or neither (A4-SR-11). The Fake News Challenge labels an article as agreeing with, disagreeing with, discussing, or unrelated to a headline (A4-SR-12). Mohammad et al. show that stance and sentiment are different signals (A4-SR-11). So a sentiment shift between versions of a claim does not by itself show a stance shift.

Fact-checking tools match repeats of a claim to an existing fact-check (A4-SR-16). Tracing a claim's origin has one formal treatment in the records: a provenance graph whose edge labels map onto three of the four reuse classes (A4-SR-15). Its authors state that they "have not solved the claim provenance problem" (A4-SR-15).

## Concept: Frame

1. **Frame as a schema of interpretation (Goffman, 1974).** A frame is a "schemata of interpretation" that a person uses "to locate, perceive, identify, and label" events (A4-SR-05, A4-SR-06; both secondary). The frame sits in the receiver's interpretation. Criticism that led to the next step: the term spread across disciplines and was "defined casually, with much left to an assumed tacit understanding" (A4-SR-07).
2. **Frame as selection and salience in a text (Entman, 1993).** "To frame is to select some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more salient in a communicating text", promoting a problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, or treatment recommendation (A4-SR-07). Criticism that led to the next step: studies of emphasis frames overlap with agenda setting and priming, so their effects "may not be exclusively attributed to framing" (Cacciatore et al. 2016 and Scheufele and Iyengar 2012, via A4-SR-08).
3. **Split into equivalence and emphasis frames (Cacciatore, Scheufele and Iyengar, 2016).** Equivalence frames present logically equivalent information. Emphasis frames "focus on different aspects of the same issue". The 2016 paper calls to "move away from studying emphasis frames" (A4-SR-08, secondary). Criticism that led to the next step: equivalent frames may not be equivalent in information, and emphasis-frame studies "tend to more accurately reflect reality" (Brüggemann and D'Angelo 2018, via A4-SR-08).
4. **Keep both types and state which one is studied (2018 onwards).** Researchers are advised to be clear about which type of frame they study, why, and how (A4-SR-08, citing Leeper and Slothuus 2018). No later criticism was recorded. This step is the current end of the sequence in the records.

Gap: Scheufele (1999) and D'Angelo (2002) sit between steps 2 and 3 in the usual account. Neither was reached in full text, so they are not steps here.

## Concept: Measuring a Frame in Text

1. **Issue-specific codebooks (before 2014).** Researchers build a codebook of frames for one issue and code documents by hand (A4-SR-09). Criticism that led to the next step: such studies "do not characterize framing at a level of abstraction that allows comparison across social issues" (A4-SR-09).
2. **Generic framing dimensions (Boydstun et al. 2014; Card et al. 2015).** Fifteen "general-purpose metaframes" are "intended to subsume all specific frames" on any issue (A4-SR-09). Annotators label spans and a primary frame per article. Criticism recorded in the same source: "A degree of subjectivity in framing analysis is unavoidable", and the same article "can cue different frames more strongly for different annotators" (A4-SR-09).
3. **Multilingual multi-label frame detection (SemEval-2023 Task 3).** Systems assign "one or more frames" from 14 of the dimensions to each article, in nine languages (A4-SR-10). The source reports Krippendorff's α of 0.342 before consolidation (A4-SR-10). Gap: A4-SR-10 does not state which criticism of step 2 led to the change. It extends the scheme to more languages and to multiple labels without saying why.

## Concept: Text Reuse Class

1. **Journalist-judged reuse classes (METER, 2002).** Documents are wholly derived, partially derived, or non-derived. Word sequences are verbatim, rewrite, or new (A4-SR-01). The scheme rests on "the intuitions of trained journalists", not on a lexical definition (A4-SR-01). Criticism that led to the next step: METER's "design does not support" plagiarism detection, and "it is an open question whether or not cases of news reuse differ from plagiarism cases" (A4-SR-02).
2. **Graded obfuscation (PAN-PC-10, 2010).** Reuse is modelled as a source passage under artificial or human obfuscation (A4-SR-02). The authors find that the kinds of reuse "do not differ very much under n-gram models" (A4-SR-02). Gap: no source in the records criticises the PAN classes in a way that led to the next news-specific step.
3. **Agency dependence by text comparison (Boumans et al., 2018).** Earlier work counted agency credit lines, "a notoriously inaccurate measurement" (A4-SR-04). The 2018 method links articles to agency copy by cosine similarity and scores verbatim reuse with a churnalism index (A4-SR-04). Criticism recorded in the same source: the agency and the newspaper may "independently of each other replicate the same source text, such as a press release", and this "cannot be ruled out entirely" (A4-SR-04).

## Concept: Stance

1. **Stance towards a target (SemEval-2016 Task 6).** A system decides whether the author is "in favor of, against, or neutral towards a proposition or target" (A4-SR-11). Criticism recorded in the same source: systems found it "markedly more difficult" when a tweet expresses opinion towards another entity (A4-SR-11). Gap: no source states that this criticism led to the next step.
2. **Stance of an article towards a headline (FNC-1, 2017).** An article agrees, disagrees, discusses, or is unrelated to a headline (A4-SR-12, secondary for FNC-1). Criticism that led to the next step: the official metric "favors the majority class" and "overestimates the true discriminative power of the methods" (A4-SR-12). Human raters reach κ = .218 on the three related classes (A4-SR-12).
3. **Class-wise and macro-averaged F1 (Hanselowski et al., 2018).** The proposed F1m metric is "not affected by the large size of the majority class" and changes the system ranking (A4-SR-12). No later criticism was recorded.

## Concept: Information Change in an Altered Claim

1. **Manual coding of claim strength (Sumner et al., 2014).** Coders rate causal claims on a seven-point scale from "correlational" to "unconditionally causal" across paper, press release, and news (A4-SR-14). Criticism that led to the next step: manual efforts are "both intractable and unattractive" at scale, and earlier studies "skip over the key first step" of pairing findings automatically (A4-SR-13).
2. **Information Matching Score (SPICED, Wright et al., 2022).** Annotators rate on five points how far the information in two findings matches, from "Completely different" to "Completely the same". They rate the information, not the wording (A4-SR-13). Models trained on the data trace change across news and tweets (A4-SR-13). Gap: no source found criticises the score for lacking a direction of change. The observation that it has no direction is this survey's reading of A4-SR-13, not a recorded criticism.

## Concept: Claim Provenance

1. **Fact-checking as a four-step pipeline (Vlachos and Riedel, 2014).** The steps are extract the statement, form questions, gather evidence, and reach a verdict (A4-SR-16, secondary). Criticism that led to the next step: verification alone leaves out "who first made a given claim, and how an original claim developed and changed over time" (A4-SR-15).
2. **Claim provenance graph (Zhang, Ives and Roth, 2020).** Edges from earlier statements to a claim carry the labels identical, paraphrased, textually entailed, or motivated, with motivated split into triggered and contradicted (A4-SR-15). Criticism recorded in the same source: the problem is not solved, and influence by "another uncited source" is left for future work (A4-SR-15).
3. **Detecting previously fact-checked claims (survey, 2021).** Repeats are "copy and paste" or "paraphrases of the original or endlessly evolving variations" and are matched to an existing fact-check (A4-SR-16). This step runs alongside step 2 rather than replacing it. Gap: the follow-up provenance paper (Zhang, Ives and Roth 2021, Politi-Prov) was not read.

## Gaps

- **Pairwise reframing.** No source found treats a change of frame between a primary report and a downstream version by another outlet as a detection task. The nearest source is MediaSpin, read from arXiv after the ojs.aaai.org PDF link returned an HTML page (A4-SR-17). It labels spin and 12 other bias types as added or removed between two versions of one headline. Both versions come from the same outlet, so it covers edits within one outlet, not reuse by another outlet (A4-SR-17). NewsEdits (Spangher et al., NAACL 2022) supplies those headline pairs and was not recorded.
- **Independent reporting.** No method separates independent reporting from undisclosed reuse of a shared source. A4-SR-04, A4-SR-14, and A4-SR-15 each name this limit.
- **Sentiment shift.** No dedicated task for sentiment shift between versions of a claim was found. A4-SR-11 shows that stance and sentiment differ, and A4-SR-14 records a related shift in claim strength.
- **Unreached primary sources.** Goffman (1974) has no open full text, so the records use two secondary sources. Cacciatore, Scheufele and Iyengar (2016): the Stanford archive copy returned HTTP 403, and archive.org blocked the request. A4-SR-08 is used instead. Scheufele (1999): only unauthorised Scribd copies were found. D'Angelo (2002), Gaizauskas et al. (2001), Johnston and Forde (2011), Boydstun et al. (2014), and the FNC-1 site were not fetched.
